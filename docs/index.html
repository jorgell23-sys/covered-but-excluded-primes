A prime that is covered — and still divides nothing

How a rule about prime numbers can be true one step at a time and false altogether. Written for someone who has never seen this before.

1. The question, with apples and oranges

Take a whole number, say 12. Its prime building blocks are 2 and 3, because 12 = 2 × 2 × 3. Now add up all the numbers that divide 12 — that is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28.

Here is the game. We ask: do the building blocks of the number also divide that sum? For 12 the blocks are 2 and 3. The sum is 28. Now 2 divides 28, but 3 does not — 28 ÷ 3 is not whole. So 12 loses the game.

Try 6 instead. Its blocks are 2 and 3, and the sum of its divisors is 1 + 2 + 3 + 6 = 12. Both 2 and 3 divide 12. So 6 wins.

The winners form a list: 6, 24, 28, 40, 54, 96, 120, … Someone catalogued it years ago. The question this page is about is a different one, and much more basic:

Which prime numbers are allowed to appear inside a winner at all?

You can play the same game with other recipes instead of "sum of divisors". This page uses a recipe that turns a prime block q raised to a power into another number, and asks the same question. And the answer has a surprise in it.

3. The recipe, step by step

Our recipe takes a prime block q raised to the power e and returns q2e − qe + 1. So:

  • block 2 to the first power → 4 − 2 + 1 = 3
  • block 7 to the first power → 49 − 7 + 1 = 43
  • block 13 to the third power → 13⁶ − 13³ + 1 = 4824613, which is 19 × 271 × 937 — so 19 divides it

To decide whether a number is a winner, you write down its blocks, apply the recipe to each one, multiply the results, and check that every block divides that product.

Now draw arrows. Put an arrow from block q to prime p whenever the recipe applied to some power of q gives a number that p divides. Then a number is a winner exactly when, in the picture of its own blocks, every block has at least one arrow coming in.

4. One you can do yourself

Is 3 covered? Apply the recipe to the block 2, first power: 2² − 2 + 1 = 3. Does 3 divide 3?

Answer

Yes. So the prime 3 is covered: there is an arrow into it, coming from 2. In fact there are many — counting only primes below 8000, exactly 511 of them send an arrow into 3.

Now the catch. Every prime that sends an arrow into 3 leaves remainder 2 when you divide it by 3 — check it with 2, 5, 11, 17, 23. And every prime that is itself covered leaves remainder 1 when divided by 6 — check with 7, 13, 19, 31. Can a prime do both?

Answer

No. A prime that leaves remainder 1 when divided by 6 also leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3, not 2. The two lists never overlap: of those 511 primes pointing at 3, exactly 0 are covered themselves.

5. Where the information comes from

Nothing on this page is an opinion. Everything was computed by the programs in src/ of this repository, and re-checked by verify.py, which anyone can run in a few seconds with nothing installed.

Two completely separate methods are used, on purpose. One works with the arrow picture. The other ignores the picture entirely: it takes every whole number up to 400000, breaks it into blocks, applies the recipe, multiplies, and divides — plain arithmetic. Where the two must agree, they are compared. They agree.

6. What was found

Back to 3. It is covered — arrows come in. But a winner is built only from primes that are themselves covered inside it, and none of the primes that point at 3 qualify. So the arrows into 3 are useless, and:

3 is covered, and it divides no winner at all.

Searching every number up to 400000 turns up 3 winners for this recipe — the smallest is 100009 = 7²·13·157 — and 0 of them are divisible by 3.

The second thing found is how many primes are shut out. That count is not random: it is decided by a symmetry group attached to the recipe, and the answer is a plain fraction. Across the recipes tested, 12 different fractions come out — so the value 1/2 seen in earlier work was a property of the two recipes looked at, not of the problem.

The third thing is where the surprise can happen at all. Sweeping 310 recipes, the covered-but-excluded primes appear 14 times, and every single one of them is a ramified prime — one where the recipe develops a repeated root. Those are finitely many for each recipe, so the surprise is rare and locatable.

And when a prime does divide a winner, you can prove it by exhibiting one: a closed loop of arrows is a winner, written out.

7. How do we know it is not wrong?

Two habits do most of the work.

A positive control

Finding zero of something proves nothing on its own — maybe the search is broken. So the same search is run on a recipe where the answer is known to be "lots". Using the sum-of-divisors-style recipe instead, the identical program finds 261 winners divisible by 3. The search works; the zero above is real.

Recomputing something published

verify.py rebuilds, from scratch, the list of numbers whose blocks divide the sum of their divisors, and compares it with the 49 terms published independently in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences as entry A175200. They match term for term. That is a check nobody here controls.

8. What it is good for

Modestly: it tells you, from the recipe alone and without any searching, which primes can never show up. If you are hunting for winners, that removes a fixed fraction of the primes from the search before it starts — and it warns you that the obvious one-step rule is not the whole rule, so a search that trusts it will look in places where nothing can be found.

9. What this does not say

  • It does not say the sum-of-divisors game has excluded primes. It does not — every prime shows up there.
  • It does not prove that the lower and upper bounds always meet. They met in every case tested, with no exception, but that is a measurement and it is written down as a conjecture, not a theorem.
  • The fraction rule is not new mathematics: it is a classical theorem of Frobenius from 1896, applied here to a new object. That is stated plainly and credited.
  • It says nothing about how many winners there are below a given size. A hypothesis about that was tested and failed, and the failure is recorded rather than hidden.

Level 2 — a textbook, with the chapter

  • K. Ireland and M. Rosen, A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory, 2nd ed., Springer. Chapter 4 for orders and roots modulo a prime; Chapter 13 for cyclotomic polynomials, which are the recipes used here.
  • B. Sury, "Frobenius and His Density Theorem for Primes", Resonance 8(12) (2003) 33–41 — a short, readable account of exactly the counting rule of section 6.

Level 3 — what this compares itself against

  • P. Stevenhagen and H. W. Lenstra, "Chebotarëv and his density theorem", Math. Intelligencer 18 (1996) 26–37. The density statement used here is theirs, not ours.

  • C. Jordan (1872) on transitive permutation groups: the reason an irreducible recipe of degree 2 or more always excludes a positive fraction of the primes.
